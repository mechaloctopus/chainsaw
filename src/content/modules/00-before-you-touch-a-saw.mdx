There is a moment, maybe ten seconds long, between walking up to a downed
tree and starting the saw. Everything that goes wrong later is already
decided in those ten seconds.

Use them.

<Block kicker="The two questions">

Before every cut. Not before every job — before every **cut**.

1. **What is holding this up?**
2. **Where does it go when it moves?**

Say both answers out loud. To your partner if you have one, to yourself if
you don't. Out loud is not theater: it is the difference between having a
thought and having a plan, and you will hear your own answer be nonsense
before your hands do anything about it.

If either answer is *I don't know* or *probably fine*, you are done. Put the
saw down and look harder, or leave it for someone who can answer.

<Hazard label="Why out loud">
A storm pile is a three-dimensional puzzle with the lights off. The limb you
are about to cut is carrying the weight of things you cannot see, and some of
those things are carrying weight of their own. Silent assumptions are how
experienced people get hurt, and they get hurt more often than beginners
because they stop saying the answers.
</Hazard>

</Block>

<Block kicker="The refusal principle">

This is the whole site in one line, so here it is up front.

<StopWork trigger="anything that makes you hesitate" who="the county, the utility, or a paid crew with the right gear">
If you don't feel safe doing it, don't do it. Flag it, keep people away from
it, and wait for someone who can.
</StopWork>

Nobody on a volunteer crew is graded on bravery. There is no award and no
scoreboard. Walking away from one tree and clearing four others is a better
day's work than the alternative, and the alternative is somebody's helicopter
ride to Wilcox.

The word for this is not *quitting*. It's **triage**. There is more work on
this island right now than any of us will finish. Spend your effort where it
is safe to spend, and hand off the rest.

</Block>

<Block kicker="What this site is">

Written reference for volunteers doing storm cleanup on Kaua‘i. Free, open,
and built to work with no signal — open it once on wifi and the whole thing
stays on your phone.

It is written for the specific job of **clearing debris after a storm**:
wood that is already down, already loaded, already tangled, sitting in mud,
full of grit, with things living in it and sometimes with wire in it.

</Block>

<Block kicker="Where to actually get trained">

Real chainsaw training exists and some of it comes to Hawai‘i. Ask around,
and ask early — the classes fill after a storm, not before.

- **NWCG S-212, Wildland Fire Chain Saws.** The standard for public-land and
  fire crews. Taught in modules with a field day and a real evaluation. If a
  local agency, fire department or conservation corps is running it, that is
  the class to get into.
- **Game of Logging.** Four levels, heavy on felling technique, bore cutting
  and hinge control. Widely regarded and genuinely good.
- **ArborMaster and other arborist training.** If your interest is trees near
  houses and lines rather than open ground, this is the direction.
- **Your local dealer.** Not training, but the people who service saws all
  day know who in your area actually knows what they're doing. Ask them.
- **A working sawyer who will let you swamp for them.** The oldest and best
  route. A day of carrying brush for someone competent teaches more than a
  week of reading.

<Note label="Check before you sign up">
Course availability on Kaua‘i changes constantly and is worse right after a
storm, when everyone wants it at once. Confirm directly with the agency or
provider — do not take a date off a web page, this one included.
</Note>

</Block>

<Block kicker="You do not need to run the saw to be useful">

Most volunteers who show up want to cut. The crew usually needs something
else more.

One competent sawyer with a good swamper clears far more than two sawyers
working alone, and does it more safely. The swamper moves every cut piece
clear, opens the work face, watches the sawyer's back and keeps the escape
path open. That job requires no saw time at all, and doing it well makes you
the person crews ask for by name.

If you are new, **that is the job to take**. Read
[the two-person saw team](/modules/10-two-person-saw-team/) and show up in
chaps and boots.

</Block>

<Block kicker="The five minutes before you start">

<Checklist id="before-you-start">
  <Check n="1" why="Both of you, every time. The swamper is in the danger zone too.">
    Two people, both in full PPE.
  </Check>
  <Check n="2" why="Not the idea of a first aid kit. A tourniquet, packing gauze, and a pressure bandage, and you have put your hands on them before.">
    A trauma kit within sixty seconds of the cut.
  </Check>
  <Check n="3" why="Knowing who calls, what you tell them, and where the vehicle is. Write the location down before you need it.">
    A comms plan, and someone who knows the address.
  </Check>
  <Check n="4" why="Walk the whole thing. Look up. Look at the ground. Look for wire.">
    A walk-around of the job before the saw comes out of the truck.
  </Check>
  <Check n="5" why="Cleared, not just noticed. Two of them, roughly back and away from where the wood will move.">
    Escape routes.
  </Check>
  <Check n="6" why="Sharp chain, working brake, chain catcher on, both caps full.">
    A saw that passed its pre-start check.
  </Check>
</Checklist>
