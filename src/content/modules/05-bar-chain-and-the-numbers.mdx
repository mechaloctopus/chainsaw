Three numbers, all three stamped on the bar. Get any one of them wrong and
the chain either will not fit or will not stay on.

<ChainNumbers />

<Block kicker="Pitch">

The distance between three consecutive rivets, divided by two. You do not
have to measure it — it is stamped on the bar — but knowing what it means
stops you buying the wrong box.

- <Spec>1/4 in</Spec> — carving and small battery saws.
- <Spec>.325 in</Spec> — mid-size saws. Fast for its size.
- <Spec>3/8 in low profile</Spec> — the homeowner standard, also sold as
  0.375 LP or Picco. Usually a low-kickback safety chain.
- <Spec>3/8 in</Spec> — the professional default, and the easiest chain to
  find on this island in a hurry.
- <Spec>.404 in</Spec> — big timber and harvester chain.

</Block>

<Block kicker="Gauge">

The thickness of the drive link — the tang that rides in the bar groove.
<Spec>.043</Spec>, <Spec>.050</Spec>, <Spec>.058</Spec>,
<Spec>.063</Spec> inches.

This one has no tolerance. Too thin and the chain rocks in the groove, cuts
crooked and throws. Too thick and it will not seat at all.

</Block>

<Block kicker="Drive link count">

How many drive links in the loop. Counted, not measured, and specific to the
bar length *and* the saw's mount. A 20-inch bar on two different saws can
take different counts.

If the bar stamp is worn away, count the drive links on the old chain. It is
tedious and it takes two minutes.

</Block>

<Block kicker="Which chain for storm cleanup">

This is the one recommendation in this module that will change your day.

**Buy semi-chisel.** The rounded working corner holds an edge in dirty wood,
and storm wood is nothing but dirty wood — root balls full of sand, trunks
that rolled through mud, salt spray on everything within a mile of the coast.

Full chisel cuts faster in clean timber and goes dull in one pass through a
muddy butt. You will spend the day filing instead of cutting.

<Block kicker="Bar maintenance">

A bar is a wear item that people treat as permanent.

<Checklist id="bar-care" ordered={false}>
  <Check id="groove" why="A scrench tip, a screwdriver, or a purpose-made groove cleaner. Packed sawdust blocks the oil feed and holds grit against the drive links.">
    **Clean the groove** and the oil holes every time the chain comes off.
  </Check>
  <Check id="flip" why="Bars wear on the bottom rail because that is the rail doing the cutting. Flipping evens it out and doubles bar life.">
    **Flip the bar** each time you fit a new chain.
  </Check>
  <Check id="dress" why="A flat file run along the rail knocks off the burr that a chain throws up. A burred rail holds the chain crooked.">
    **Dress the rails** when you feel a burr.
  </Check>
  <Check id="spread" why="Squeeze the rails together with your fingers at the middle of the bar. If a drive link rocks noticeably side to side, the rails are spread and the bar is finished.">
    **Check for rail spread.**
  </Check>
  <Check id="nose" why="Nose sprockets seize when the oil feed blocks. A sprocket-nose bar that stops turning will strip a chain.">
    **Spin the nose sprocket** if you have one.
  </Check>
</Checklist>

</Block>

<Hazard label="Too much bar">
A bar longer than the saw was designed for is not a bigger saw. It is a saw
that bogs in the cut, tempts you to lean on it, puts the kickback quadrant
further from your hands, and oils badly at the nose because the pump was
never sized for it.

If you need a bigger cut, get a bigger saw or make the cut in stages from
both sides.
</Hazard>
