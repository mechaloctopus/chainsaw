A sharp chain cuts itself. You guide it. A dull chain has to be pushed, and
pushing is how the bar ends up somewhere you did not plan.

Learn this on a bench, at home, with a chain in a vise, before you try it on
a tailgate in the rain.

<Block kicker="What you are actually making">

A cutter is a small knife blade bent over at the top, and the part doing the
work is the **corner** where the top plate meets the side plate. Everything
about filing is about that corner: how sharp it is, what angle it meets the
wood at, and how deep a bite the depth gauge in front of it allows.

<Block kicker="The numbers, and where they come from">

- **Round file diameter** by pitch:
  <Spec unverified>.325 → 3/16 in</Spec>,
  <Spec unverified>3/8 LP → 5/32 in</Spec>,
  <Spec unverified>3/8 → 7/32 in</Spec>,
  <Spec unverified>.404 → 7/32 in</Spec>.
  These vary between Oregon, STIHL and Carlton — match the file to the chain
  you own.
- **Top-plate angle:** around <Spec unverified>25°–35°</Spec>, commonly 30°.
  Most chains have a witness mark stamped on the top plate. Follow the mark.
- **File height:** roughly a fifth of the file diameter should sit above the
  top plate. Too low and you get hook; too high and you get back-slope.
- **Side-plate angle:** set by holding the file level, or at the small
  downward angle the manufacturer specifies for that chain.
- **Depth gauges:** around <Spec unverified>.025 in</Spec> for general use,
  set with a gauge tool made for your chain.

These are working figures, and this site does not publish a spec it has not
checked against the manufacturer. Your chain box, or the manufacturer's
filing chart, is the authority. See the [unverified list](/about/verify/).
</Note>

</Block>

<Block kicker="Hook and back-slope">

The two ways the top plate goes wrong, and they feel completely different in
the wood.

- **Too much hook.** The file sat too low, undercutting the edge. The chain
  grabs, chatters, and pulls itself into the cut. Corners break off. It feels
  aggressive and dangerous, and it is.
- **Back-slope.** The file sat too high, so the edge leans away from the wood.
  The chain will not cut, the saw smokes, and you push harder — which is the
  most common way people end up in trouble.

Both come from file height, not from the angle, which is why a guide is worth
using until your hands know the height.

</Block>

<Block kicker="Every tooth the same length">

This is the rule beginners skip and the reason their saws cut in an arc.

If the cutters on one side are longer than the other, that side takes a bigger
bite, and the saw curves away in the cut — no matter how you hold it. Once you
have hit a rock with one cutter and filed it back hard, you have to bring
**every** cutter down to match it.

Count your strokes. Same number, same angle, same height, every tooth, both
sides. Then check the lengths by eye against the shortest one.

</Block>

<Block kicker="Rakers, and why they sneak up on you">

The depth gauge — the raker — is the little ramp in front of each cutter. It
decides how deep the corner can bite.

Every time you file a cutter, the cutter gets shorter and lower. The raker
does not. So after a few sharpenings the rakers are effectively **too high**,
the cutters cannot reach the wood, and the saw stops cutting even though every
edge is razor sharp.

1. Sharpen the cutters first.
2. Lay the gauge tool across the chain.
3. File the part of the raker that stands above the tool, flat, with a flat
   file.
4. **Round off the front of the raker** afterwards so it does not catch.
5. Check them every two or three sharpenings, not every one.

<Hazard label="Rakers too low">
Filing rakers too far is the fastest way to make a dangerous chain. It takes
an enormous bite, grabs, and kicks. If the saw suddenly feels violent and
self-feeding after a sharpening session, that is what you did.
</Hazard>

</Block>

<Block kicker="Jig or freehand">

Both. In that order.

**Learn on a jig.** A roller guide, a bar-mounted filing guide, or a bench
grinder gives you repeatability you do not have yet — consistent angle,
consistent height, consistent tooth length. For a volunteer crew where
several people share saws, a jig is the right call, because it makes the
result independent of whose hands are on the file.

**Then learn freehand.** It is faster, it needs nothing but a file and a
gauge, and it is what you will actually do on a tailgate at 2pm with four
chains to bring back. But freehand needs the muscle memory that filing a
hundred teeth in a jig gives you.

</Block>

<Block kicker="Diagnose from the chips">

The wood tells you everything. Look at what comes out of the cut.

| What you see | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Big square chips, saw pulls itself in | Sharp. This is what you want. |
| Fine dust, saw needs pushing | Dull, or rakers too high. |
| Long stringy shavings | Chain is fine; you are cutting green softwood fiber — albizia does this. |
| The cut curves off square | One side filed harder or longer than the other. |
| Smoke, hot bar, chain slack | Dull, dirty, or the oiler has stopped. |
| Chattering, grabbing | Too much hook, or rakers filed too low. |

</Block>

<FailureModes>
  <Fail tell="Saw cuts fine for two tanks after sharpening then 'goes dull' fast. The edges are fine — the rakers were never touched.">
    Sharpening cutters and never touching the depth gauges.
  </Fail>
  <Fail tell="The cut wanders and you find yourself twisting the saw to correct it. Measure your cutters — one side will be visibly longer.">
    Filing one side harder than the other.
  </Fail>
  <Fail tell="Fresh file marks on the raker that you never rounded off. It catches on the wood and the chain jerks.">
    Leaving a square front edge on a filed raker.
  </Fail>
  <Fail tell="You are filing in the field, in the rain, on a tailgate, with a chain you could have swapped in three minutes. Twenty minutes lost, badly filed.">
    Filing when you should be swapping.
  </Fail>
</FailureModes>
