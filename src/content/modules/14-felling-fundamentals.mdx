**Most storm cleanup work is not felling.** It is bucking and limbing wood
that is already on the ground, and that is where a volunteer crew adds the
most value with the least risk.

If a tree needs to come down near a structure, a road, a vehicle or a power
line, **it is not a volunteer job.** Not with a rope, not with a truck, not
with a plan. That work needs an insured outfit with the right equipment and
someone who does it every week.

This module exists so that you can look at a felling job and know, with
specifics, why you are saying no — and so that if you do fell an open-ground
tree, you understand what is steering it.

<Hazard label="Clear the smalls means cleared">
Brush around the stump catches the bar, hides your feet, and turns an escape
route into a trip hazard at the exact moment you need to run on it.

This is the step everybody shortcuts. Fifteen minutes of brushing before a
fell is not optional work — it is the work.
</Hazard>

</Block>

<Block kicker="Nobody in the drop zone">

Verbal and visual all-clear, every time, out loud.

- **Two tree lengths minimum** for bystanders, in every direction — not just
  the direction you think it is going.
- **Call it out.** Loud, before the back cut: the tree, the direction, and
  the fact that you are cutting.
- **Get an answer.** Silence is not confirmation. Your swamper confirms the
  drop zone is clear and stays where you can see him.
- **You do not start** if there is anyone in the zone you cannot personally
  see with your own eyes.

</Block>

<Block kicker="The hinge, and what it does">

<HingeFace />

A falling tree is steered by an untouched strip of wood called the **hinge**
(or holding wood), left between the face cut and the back cut.

The hinge bends as the tree goes over, and while it is bending it holds the
trunk in line with the direction the face cut opens. That is the entire
steering mechanism. There is nothing else.

- **Even thickness, all the way across.** A hinge thicker on one side steers
  the tree toward the thick side.
- **Roughly 10% of the tree's diameter.** Thicker and it may not break; thinner
  and it tears through early.
- **Never cut through the hinge.** A tree with no hinge does exactly what its
  lean and the wind tell it to, which may be backwards.
- **Never cut off a corner of it.** The corners are what hold the trunk
  against sideways movement.

</Block>

<Block kicker="The face cut">

The notch you remove from the side you want the tree to fall toward. The two
cuts must **meet cleanly** at the back of the notch.

- **Open face**, around <Spec unverified>70°</Spec>. A wide notch, so the
  hinge stays intact and steering for most of the fall. The modern
  recommendation in most training.
- **Conventional**, around <Spec unverified>45°</Spec>. Closes earlier in the
  fall, at which point the hinge breaks and steering stops.
- **Humboldt.** The sloping cut is on the underside — the notch is cut up into
  the stump rather than down into the log. Saves the butt log, used in big
  timber.

<Hazard label="A bypassed face is a failure, not a blemish">
If the two face cuts do not meet — if one runs past the other and leaves a
step of uncut wood in the corner — you have a **dutchman**. It grabs one side
of the hinge as the tree starts to move and throws the tree off line,
sometimes hard and sideways.

There is no "close enough." Recut the face until it meets clean, and if that
means opening the notch wider, open it wider.
</Hazard>

</Block>

<Block kicker="The back cut">

The cut that releases the tree, made from the opposite side, leaving the
hinge.

- Level with, or slightly above, the apex of the face cut, depending on the
  face type — check what the technique you were taught calls for.
- **Come in slowly and stop before you are committed.** Set your wedges while
  you still have wood holding the tree.
- Watch the kerf, not the bar. A kerf that starts to open is the tree
  committing; a kerf that starts to close on you is a back-leaner and you
  should already be wedging.
- Watch and listen. Cracking means it is moving.
- When it goes: **brake the saw, step back along your escape route, and keep
  looking up.**

</Block>

<Block kicker="Wedges">

- **Plastic only.** Never steel, never anywhere a chain might touch it.
- **Two are better than one.** They share the load and they stop the tree
  sitting back on the bar.
- Set them in the back cut before you are committed, and drive them with a
  hammer or the back of an axe.
- They do two jobs: keeping the cut from closing on your bar, and giving a
  slight back-leaner a push in the intended direction.

</Block>

<Block kicker="Barber chair">

The failure mode that kills fallers.

**What it is.** The trunk splits vertically up the middle as it starts to go,
and the front half of the butt rears up and back off the stump — into the
space the operator is standing in, at the height of their chest and head.

**What causes it.** A tree that leans away from the direction of fall, combined
with a back cut made too fast, with no hinge control and no bore cut. Brittle
wood makes it far more likely — which on this island means **albizia**.

**How to avoid it.** Do not fell back-leaners with a conventional back cut.
Use the bore-cut method so the tree is never carrying load on an unsupported
strap. Keep a proper hinge. Wedge early. And when in doubt, do not fell it.

</Block>

<Block kicker="The bore cut, at recognition level only">

For a back-leaner, the technique is to bore the bar through the trunk behind
the hinge, establish the hinge from the inside, leave a holding strap of wood
at the back, wedge, and then cut the strap last so the tree is released only
when everything else is set.

**This site is not going to teach you to do that.** It is a nose-first
technique on a loaded trunk, and the consequences of getting it wrong are
exactly the failure described above. It is taught hands-on, with an instructor,
on trees that do not matter.

What you should take from it: **if a tree leans away from where it has to go,
it needs a technique you have not been taught, and it is not your tree
today.**

</Block>

<Block kicker="After the fall">

The moment it hits the ground is not the end.

1. **Look up.** Things came loose on the way down and are still coming.
2. **Move.** Do not walk straight to the stump.
3. **Wait.** Give it a few seconds. Loaded material settles.
4. **Reassess.** The tree on the ground is now a completely new job with new
   binds, a new root ball and possibly a spring pole it made on the way down.
5. **Then approach** — from the butt, uphill, looking up.

</Block>
