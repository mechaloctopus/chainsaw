<Hazard label="Read this first">
**Assume every downed line is energized. Always.**

Stay at least **30 feet** away and call **911**. Then report it to **KIUC at
808-246-4300**.

A line can re-energize without warning from an automatic recloser or from a
neighbor's generator backfeeding the circuit.
</Hazard>

<Contacts />

<Block kicker="The rule, and there is no version of it with an exception">

**Never cut a tree or limb that is touching a line, near a line, or that
could fall into a line.**

Not with a rope. Not with a pole saw. Not from a ladder. Not "just this one
branch to get it off the driveway." Not because the power is out in the
neighborhood. Not because someone says it's a phone line.

This is not a skill problem, and it is not about how good you are. It is a
utility job, with utility equipment, done by people who can confirm the
circuit is dead. Your part is to keep everyone away from it and make the
call.

<StopWork trigger="a line in the job" who="KIUC and the county">
A line down is the end of your work in that area, not a complication to work
around. Mark it, guard it, report it, and go clear something else.
</StopWork>

</Block>

<Block kicker="Thirty feet">

Thirty feet is further than it feels. Most people stop at about twelve and
believe they are at thirty.

<ThirtyFeet />

That distance is the minimum for a line lying still. If a limb moves, if a
truck brushes the debris, if the wind gets into the crown, the line moves
too — and it does not move gently. Give it more than the minimum whenever you
can.

</Block>

<Block kicker="What it looks like in a storm pile">

A downed line in open grass is easy. Almost none of them are in open grass.

- **In the crown.** A conductor dragged down with the tree, threaded through
  the branches, the same dull gray as everything else and invisible from
  twenty feet. This is the one that kills people who "checked."
- **Under tension.** The line is stretched between a pole and the tree that
  pulled it down. Remove a limb and it whips, or the pole comes back, or the
  span snaps.
- **In wet grass and standing water.** Water spreads the energy outward.
  There is no safe way to judge how far.
- **Service drops.** The line from the pole to a house is a conductor too,
  and it is the one most often pulled down by a single limb. A house with the
  lights off tells you nothing — the drop can still be live from the utility
  side, or made live by a generator inside.
- **Things that became conductors.** Chain-link fence, barbed wire, guy
  wires, rain gutters, metal roofing, a wet rope, a wire clothesline. The
  contact point can be a hundred feet from where you are standing.
- **Communications lines that are not.** Cable and phone lines hang lower and
  look different — thicker, often with a messenger wire. Do not bet your
  hands on being able to tell. Treat all of it as energized.

<Hazard label="Termite and rot damage">
On this island a lot of poles and a lot of trunk wood is not as sound as it
looks. A pole with a rotten base can come over hours after the storm, with no
wind, while you are working under the span. Look at the base of the pole, not
just the wire.
</Hazard>

</Block>

<Block kicker="Step potential — how the ground itself hurts you">

Energy leaving a downed conductor spreads out through the ground in rings,
and the voltage drops as it goes. If your two feet are in two different
rings, current flows up one leg and down the other.

This is why the instruction is specific:

1. **Do not run.** A stride puts maximum distance between your feet, which is
   exactly the wrong thing.
2. **Put your feet together.** Heels and toes touching.
3. **Shuffle.** Small steps, both feet sliding, never losing contact with each
   other, never lifting far off the ground.
4. **Keep going** well past thirty feet before you stand normally.

You cannot see, hear or smell a ground gradient. Tingling in your feet means
you are already in it — shuffle out, do not investigate.

</Block>

<Block kicker="If a line falls on a vehicle">

<Checklist id="line-on-vehicle" ordered={false}>
  <Check id="stay" why="The tires and the body are not what protect you — being inside one conductive shell is. Getting out is the dangerous part.">
    **Stay in the vehicle.** Tell anyone else inside to stay put.
  </Check>
  <Check id="call" why="Give the location and say there is a line on an occupied vehicle. That changes the response.">
    Call 911 from inside.
  </Check>
  <Check id="warn" why="A bystander walking over to help is the most common way this injures a second person.">
    Wave people off. Shout, do not let anyone approach.
  </Check>
  <Check id="wait" why="Not the fire department, not a neighbor with gloves. The utility.">
    Wait for the utility to confirm the line is dead.
  </Check>
  <Check id="fire" why="Open the door, do not touch the vehicle and the ground at the same time, jump clear with both feet together, land with feet together, and shuffle away.">
    Only get out if the vehicle is on fire — and then **jump clear**, touching nothing.
  </Check>
</Checklist>

</Block>

<Block kicker="Generator backfeed">

A generator wired into a house without a transfer switch — an extension cord
into a dryer outlet, a "suicide cord," a panel breaker flipped by hand — pushes
power back out through the service drop and into the line outside.

That line is now live even though the utility circuit is dead, and the
utility does not know about it.

Which means: **a dead line and a live line look identical, and the person who
made it live is two houses away and has no idea you are there.** This is the
single best reason the rule is *always*, with no exception for "the power is
out."

</Block>

<Block kicker="Mark it and guard it" >

Reporting is not the end of it. A line in a debris field will be found by a
kid on a bike before it is found by a crew.

- From a safe distance, block the approach. Sawhorses, cones, road spikes of
  brush, a truck parked across the path, flagging tape strung between two
  trees well back from the line.
- Tell the neighbors. Actually knock. Point at it, say what it is, say
  thirty feet.
- Do not leave it unmarked because you reported it. Response after a
  hurricane takes days, and KIUC has around 1,500 miles of line to inspect.
- If you can safely stay until someone arrives, and it is in a place where
  people walk, stay.

</Block>

<FailureModes>
  <Fail tell="Somebody says 'the power's been out since Monday.' That sentence has killed people. Reclosers re-energize automatically, crews re-energize sections as they repair, and generators backfeed.">
    Treating an outage as proof a line is dead.
  </Fail>
  <Fail tell="You're standing in the crown and you can see the wire behind you but not where it goes. If you can't trace both ends, you don't know what's under tension.">
    Working in a crown with a line in it because it "looks clear on this side."
  </Fail>
  <Fail tell="The limb comes off and the whole span moves. You planned for the limb, not for the wire it was holding down.">
    Taking one limb off a tree that is holding a line down.
  </Fail>
  <Fail tell="A pole saw is fiberglass, and fiberglass conducts when it is wet, dirty, or has a wet rope on it. Nothing in a volunteer's kit is rated for live work.">
    Believing a fiberglass pole, a dry rope or rubber boots make it safe.
  </Fail>
  <Fail tell="Your feet tingle, or the grass looks wrong, or you notice you're on wet ground. Stop, feet together, shuffle out.">
    Walking a normal stride out of an energized area.
  </Fail>
</FailureModes>

and required.
</Note>
