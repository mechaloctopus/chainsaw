A chainsaw does not cut you. It **removes** a piece of you, at around sixty
feet per second, in about a fiftieth of a second. There is no reflex fast
enough. Everything protective has to already be on.

<Block kicker="Head to foot">

<Checklist id="ppe">
  <Check n="1" why="A hard hat for what falls, a face screen for what flies, and both of them on before you walk into the work area, not when you start the saw.">
    **Head and face.** Helmet with a mesh face screen.
  </Check>
  <Check n="2" why="Safety glasses under the screen. The screen stops chips; it does not stop the fine dust and the grit that comes off a muddy root ball.">
    **Eyes.** Glasses as well as the screen.
  </Check>
  <Check n="3" why="A saw at your ear is loud enough to take your hearing permanently, and it takes it a little at a time so you never notice the day it happened. Muffs let you hear shouted words; plugs muffle them.">
    **Ears.** Muffs or plugs, worn the whole time the saw runs.
  </Check>
  <Check n="4" why="Grip first, cut protection second. Wet, oily gloves that slip are worse than bare hands, and a loose cuff catches.">
    **Hands.** Gloves that fit.
  </Check>
  <Check n="5" why="Chaps or chainsaw trousers meeting ASTM F1897, or UL certified. That standard is the whole point of buying them — a canvas apron does nothing.">
    **Legs.** Chaps, front and side coverage, down to the top of the boot.
  </Check>
  <Check n="6" why="Steel or composite toe, high ankle, aggressive sole. Storm ground is mud, loose bark and rolling rounds. Rubber boots are not saw boots.">
    **Feet.** Boots with a toe cap and real tread.
  </Check>
  <Check n="7" why="Long sleeves and long trousers that fit close. Loose clothing is something for a chain to catch, and bare arms in a tropical crown means cuts, sap, bees and centipedes.">
    **Body.** Close-fitting long sleeves and trousers.
  </Check>
</Checklist>

</Block>

<Block kicker="How chaps actually work">

They do not armor your leg. They jam the saw.

The outer shell is tough, but the layers behind it are a loose mat of long
ballistic fibers. When the chain cuts through the shell it pulls those fibers
out, and they wrap the sprocket and pack the clutch cover in a fraction of a
second. The chain stops because the saw stops.

That mechanism has consequences, and they are the part people don't know:

<FailureModes kicker="What breaks the mechanism">
  <Fail tell="A cut, a scuff through the shell, oil soaking, or fibers visible at the edge of a scuff. Chaps are single-use in the place they were cut — retire them, don't patch them.">
    **Damaged or contaminated chaps.** Fibers clogged with oil and sawdust do
    not pull free and do not jam anything.
  </Fail>
  <Fail tell="Chaps twisted around your leg so the panel is on the inside or the back. The protection is where the panel is, and the panel belongs on the front and outside.">
    **Worn slack or rotated.** They have to sit where the bar will arrive.
  </Fail>
  <Fail tell="Coverage that stops at mid-calf. The most common serious leg wound is low — the shin and the top of the foot, from limbing with the bar near your legs.">
    **Too short.** They need to reach the boot.
  </Fail>
  <Fail tell="An electric or battery saw with a high-torque motor may keep driving the chain as the fibers load up, where a two-stroke would stall. Treat chaps as less reliable with electric saws, not more.">
    **Assuming the same protection on a battery saw.** The jamming effect
    depends on stalling the drive.
  </Fail>
</FailureModes>

<Note label="Buy by standard, not by brand">
Ask for chaps that meet **ASTM F1897** or that are **UL certified**, and check
the label is still legible on the pair you own. That is the whole
specification. Everything after that — six layer or nine layer, wrap or apron,
buckle style — is fit and comfort, and comfort matters mainly because
uncomfortable chaps end up in the truck.
</Note>

</Block>

<Block kicker="Both people are chapped. Every time.">

The swamper is not a spectator. He is inside arm's reach of a running saw,
bent over, with his hands in the same wood.

The sawyer is the one who gets hurt least often per hour. The person who
walks in to drag a piece clear at the wrong moment is a different story.

Full PPE for everyone inside the work area. If you have three people and two
sets of chaps, you have two people working and one person keeping bystanders
back — not three people working.

</Block>

<Block kicker="The kit that matters">

A chainsaw wound is an arterial bleed with a ragged, contaminated edge.
Nobody is putting a plaster on it. The kit has to be a **trauma** kit, it has
to be **sixty seconds away from the cut**, and you have to have opened one
before today.

<Checklist id="trauma-kit" ordered={false}>
  <Check id="tq" why="A real one — CAT, SOFTT-W, SAM-XT. Not a rubber band, not an improvised belt. One each, carried on the person, not in the truck.">
    **Tourniquet ×2.**
  </Check>
  <Check id="gauze" why="For wounds a tourniquet can't reach — groin, armpit, shoulder, neck. Packing is a technique: you press gauze into the wound, hard, and keep pressing.">
    **Hemostatic or plain packing gauze.**
  </Check>
  <Check id="bandage" why="An Israeli-style pressure bandage puts and keeps pressure on with one hand.">
    **Pressure bandage.**
  </Check>
  <Check id="gloves" why="Nitrile gloves and shears. You will be cutting through chaps and a boot.">
    **Gloves and trauma shears.**
  </Check>
  <Check id="phone" why="Charged, and a written note of where you are. Not a dropped pin you can't send with no bars.">
    **A phone, and the location written down.**
  </Check>
  <Check id="skill" why="Wound packing and tourniquet application are skills, not objects. Stop the Bleed classes are short, cheap and often free. Take one, and take your partner.">
    **Someone on site who has been taught to use all of it.**
  </Check>
</Checklist>

<Hazard label="Where it goes">
On your body or on the saw, not in the truck at the bottom of the driveway.
The person bleeding cannot walk to it and the person helping cannot leave.
</Hazard>

</Block>

<Block kicker="The buddy rule">

**No solo saw work.** Not for one cut, not for five minutes, not in your own
yard.

The reason is not company. It is that the failure mode of chainsaw work is a
bleed that becomes fatal in a couple of minutes, and every single step of
managing it — the tourniquet, the call, the drive out — needs a second pair of
hands.

Working alone also removes the thing that makes crews safe: someone whose
only job, at that moment, is to look at what you are doing from outside it.

</Block>

<Block kicker="The comms plan, decided before the saw starts">

Say these out loud, as a crew, once:

1. **Who calls 911?** Name the person.
2. **What do you tell them?** The location in terms a dispatcher can route:
   road name, nearest cross street or mile marker, landmark, gate color.
   Post-storm, the driveway you came up may not exist on a map.
3. **Is there signal here?** Check, at the start. If there isn't, where is the
   nearest point that has it, and who goes?
4. **Who drives out?** Which vehicle, where is it parked, are the keys in it,
   and is the route out still clear or did you just drop a tree across it?
5. **What is the stop-work signal?** One that works over a running saw — a
   flat hand held up, repeated, until it is seen.

<Hazard label="Heat, water and the 2pm problem">
Most bad cuts happen when people are tired, and on this island tired arrives
early. Heat, humidity, full PPE and a fifteen-pound saw will cook you by
early afternoon.

Water in the work area, not in the truck. Salt as well as water if you're
sweating through your shirt. Rotate roles every tank, which forces a rest
whether you want one or not. And when someone starts making sloppy cuts,
that's the signal to stop — not to push through the last few.
</Hazard>

</Block>
