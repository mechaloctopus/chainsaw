Everything in the other modules assumes wood that is sitting still. Storm
wood is not sitting still. It is a pile of loaded springs that happen to be
holding each other in place, and the energy in it was put there by a
hurricane.

<Block kicker="What is holding this up?">

The first of the two questions, and the answer is always one of two things:
the wood is being **squeezed** somewhere, and **pulled apart** somewhere else.

- **Compression side** — the wood is being crushed. Cut into it and the kerf
  closes on your bar.
- **Tension side** — the wood is being pulled apart. Cut into it and it splits
  open and the piece drops, fast.

Cut the **compression side first**, then finish from the tension side. Or bore
through the middle and cut outward. Get the order backwards and you either
pinch the saw or get the piece dropping while your bar is still in it.

<BindOrder />

<Hazard label="The classic trap">
A log lying across a yard that looks like it is flat on the ground. It is
not. It is bridging over a dip, or resting on a rock, or held up at both ends
by its own branches.

Buck it in the middle like it is on the ground, and the two halves come down
and the middle comes up. You are standing between them.

**Test it.** Push on it. Look along its length at ground level. Look for where
the branches underneath are bent — those are the supports.
</Hazard>

</Block>

<Block kicker="Spring poles">

A sapling or a limb bent double under something heavier, storing the energy
of the bend. Common wherever a big tree came down through undergrowth.

<SpringPole />

1. **Identify it.** Anything bowed into a curve it did not grow in is loaded.
2. **Find the apex** — the tightest point of the bend. That is where the load
   is concentrated.
3. **Stand outside the arc** it will sweep when it releases. Look at the curve
   and picture where the tip goes. Do not stand there.
4. **Shave the outside of the bend** — the convex, stretched side — with small
   shallow cuts, an inch of wood at a time, at or near the apex.
5. It will release itself progressively as you shave. That is the point: you
   are letting the energy out gradually instead of all at once.

**Never** cut through a spring pole in one pass, and never cut the inside of
the bend.

</Block>

<Block kicker="Root balls and rootwads">

A partly uprooted tree has torn its root plate half out of the ground and is
now standing on a hinge of remaining roots, with the weight of the trunk
holding it over.

When you sever the trunk, you remove the weight — and the root plate slams
back into the hole it came out of, because that is where the tension in the
remaining roots is pulling it.

<Hazard label="The geometry that gets people">
The dangerous place is the **inside** of the root ball — the hollow where the
plate came up, on the side the plate will rotate back toward. That is where
you stand to reach the trunk, and it is where the plate goes.

Never work on the inside or the downhill side of a root ball. Cut the trunk
from the far side, well away from the plate, and take the butt section off in
pieces from a distance.
</Hazard>

Root balls also bring up rock, fence wire, buried concrete and irrigation
pipe. Assume the first foot of the butt log has metal in it and cut
accordingly — or do not cut it at all.

</Block>

<Block kicker="Widow makers and hangers">

Broken limbs and snapped tops caught in the canopy, and tangled crowns
holding pieces that are no longer attached to anything.

They come down on their own schedule: a gust, a vibration, the thump of the
round you just dropped, or nothing at all.

- **Look up before you start.** Then look up again during, and again after
  every piece moves. It is a habit, not a step.
- A hanger above the work area is a **stop work** until it is brought down
  safely or the area under it is roped off and abandoned.
- You cannot "work quickly underneath it."
- You cannot reliably knock one down with a thrown rope or a pole, and the
  attempt puts you under it.

<StopWork trigger="a hanger above the work area" who="an arborist with a rope and a saddle, or a crew with a lift">
Rope off the ground under it, put something visible at the edge, tell the
homeowner what it is, and work somewhere else.
</StopWork>

</Block>

<Block kicker="Trees hung up in other trees">

A tree that fell partway and is leaning on a standing tree, or caught in
another crown, and has not reached the ground.

**This is a refuse-the-job item for volunteers. Full stop.**

The reasons are not about skill: the tree is loaded in directions you cannot
read, it can roll or slide sideways rather than falling forward, the tree it
is leaning on is also now loaded and can fail, and the standard techniques
for dealing with it need winches, cables and training none of us have on a
Saturday.

Professionals dislike these. That should tell you enough.

</Block>

<Block kicker="Reading rot by sound">

On this island a tree that looks healthy can be a shell. Termites, fungal
rot, and a growing season that never stops mean a trunk can be hollow behind
solid-looking bark.

**The sound test.** Strike the trunk with the back of an axe or a hammer,
firmly, in several places up and around it.

- A **solid ring** — a sharp, high, short sound — is sound wood.
- A **dull hollow thud**, or a drum note, is a void.

Do it on a tree you know is solid first so you know what you are listening
for.

**And the visual signs**, which matter as much:

- Conks and bracket fungi on the trunk — fruiting bodies of a rot that is
  already well established inside.
- Cat faces, old wounds, and seams running up the trunk.
- Insect activity, boreholes, frass, and **termite mud tubes**.
- A dead top, or a section of crown with no leaves.
- Bulges, swellings, or bark that sounds and feels soft under a thumb.
- A trunk that is visibly out of round.

<StopWork trigger="a hollow trunk" who="an arborist">
A hollow or heavily rotted trunk does not behave like wood. It has no hinge
strength, which means it cannot be steered, and it can fail in any direction
at any point in the cut, including backwards.
</StopWork>

</Block>

<Block kicker="The species you will actually meet">

<Species name="Albizia" latin="Falcataria moluccana" verdict="Treat every albizia as unpredictable. This is the tree that will hurt you.">
The fastest growing tree on the island and the most brittle. It puts on
enormous mass in a few years with wood that has almost no strength for its
size, which means limbs the size of a truck sitting on nothing, holding
themselves up on fiber that is already broken.

It drops limbs with no warning and no sound, in still air. It splits and
barber-chairs readily because the wood has no tension strength. A fallen
albizia crown is a lattice of pieces that are no longer attached, resting on
each other.

The wood is also pale, soft and wet, which fools people into thinking it is
easy cutting. It is easy cutting. That is not the problem.
</Species>

<Species name="Ironwood" latin="Casuarina equisetifolia" verdict="Hard, heavy, and it dulls chains fast. Shallow rooted in sand.">
Dense wood that cuts slowly and hard, often with sand in the bark from
coastal wind. Frequently comes over as a whole tree in saturated ground
rather than breaking, because the root plate is shallow and wide — which means
root ball hazards, not limb hazards. Stands of them drop needles that make a
slick, deep, footing-free mat.
</Species>

<Species name="Java plum" latin="Syzygium cumini" verdict="Heavy, dense crowns that hold hangers well out of reach.">
Big, dense, multi-stemmed crowns that break into tangles rather than falling
clear. The tangles hold broken pieces high and out of view, and the wood is
heavy enough that a modest-looking limb is a serious weight. Often grown
through fences and old plantation wire.
</Species>

<Species name="Monkeypod" latin="Samanea saman" verdict="Enormous horizontal limbs, huge lever arms, and included bark at the unions.">
A wide, spreading crown with long horizontal limbs, which means a limb's
weight acts a very long way from where you are cutting. Unions often have
included bark and split under storm load. When one of these fails it takes
out fences, roofs and vehicles, and the failed part stays under load on the
ground.
</Species>

<Species name="Mango" latin="Mangifera indica" verdict="Rots from old pruning wounds. Sound it before you trust it.">
Commonly old, commonly pruned badly for decades, and commonly rotten in the
main unions where those cuts were made. The sound test earns its keep here.
Heavy, dense wood; sappy, sticky cuts.
</Species>

<Species name="Coconut palm" latin="Cocos nucifera" verdict="Not wood. Do not treat it like wood." >
Palm is fibrous, stringy and wet. It does not have a hinge, it does not have
rings, it does not split predictably, and it binds and grabs chains. A palm
does not fall the way a tree falls. Standing palms after a storm also hold
loose fronds and nuts thirty feet up — everything under them is a drop zone.
</Species>

</Block>

<Block kicker="Ground, water and what lives in the pile">

- **Saturated ground and mud.** Footing first: if you cannot stand still
  comfortably, you cannot control a saw. Mud also means root plates are
  loose — trees that survived the wind come over days later in still air.
- **Slopes.** Work from the uphill side, and know what rolls downhill when you
  cut it loose. Never below a round that can roll.
- **Streambanks and undercut ground.** Post-flood water takes the soil out
  from under root systems while leaving the surface looking normal. The ground
  you are standing on may be a lid.
- **Bees and wasps.** Nests in cavities and in the ground, and a running saw
  is a vibration source that provokes them. Look before you cut, and know if
  anyone on the crew carries an epi-pen.
- **Centipedes and what else is in a downed crown.** Gloves on, and do not
  put a bare hand into a pile.
- **Wire, nails and cable.** Plantation-era trees and fence-line trees have
  metal grown into them — old fencing, staples, cable, guy wire. Look at the
  bark for the tell-tale swelling where a wire went in, and expect metal
  within a few feet of any old fence.

</Block>
