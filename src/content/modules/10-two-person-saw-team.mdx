This is the most important module on this site that is not about power lines,
and it is the one most volunteer crews get wrong — usually by being two people
who each brought a saw.

Two sawyers working separately is not a crew. It is two people each working
alone, twenty feet apart, with nobody watching either of them.

<Block kicker="The shape of it">

- **Two people.** Both in full PPE. Both able to run the saw. Both able to
  swamp.
- **One saw running at a time.** One sawyer, one swamper.
- **Roles swap every time the tank runs dry.**

That is the whole method. Everything below is why.

</Block>

<Block kicker="Tank-for-tank rotation">

When the saw runs out of fuel, you swap. The sawyer becomes the swamper and
the swamper becomes the sawyer.

It sounds like a scheduling detail. It does four things, and each one of them
is worth more than it looks.

1. **It forces a rest.** A tank is twenty to thirty minutes of saw work. Nobody
   in full PPE in Hawai‘i heat should be doing more than that without
   stopping, and nobody will stop voluntarily while the pile is still there.
2. **It keeps both skill sets live.** The person who has not touched the saw
   all day is the person who cannot take over when the other one gets hurt,
   overheats, or has to drive out.
3. **It creates a mandatory re-assessment.** Every twenty to thirty minutes,
   work stops, both people step back, and the job gets looked at fresh. The
   pile has moved since you started. So has the load in the log.
4. **It breaks the tunnel.** Two hours into a job a sawyer stops seeing the
   whole tree and starts seeing only the next cut. Swapping roles physically
   removes you from that.

</Block>

<Block kicker="The swamper's job">

The swamper is not a spectator with a rake. This is the job that determines
how safe and how fast the whole operation is.

<Checklist id="swamper" ordered={false}>
  <Check id="clear" why="Every piece, as it falls, before the next cut if you can. A sawyer standing in his own cut pieces has no footing and no escape route.">
    **Move every cut piece clear.**
  </Check>
  <Check id="piles" why="Burn, chip, haul, keep. Sorted as you go, because sorting a mixed pile afterwards is a second full day. Know the county's current green waste rules before you build the piles.">
    **Stack in dedicated piles.**
  </Check>
  <Check id="face" why="Brush cut and dragged away so the sawyer can see the whole job and step forward into clear ground. An open work face is the difference between reading the tree and guessing.">
    **Open up the work face.**
  </Check>
  <Check id="escape" why="It was clear when you started. It is where you have been throwing brush for twenty minutes. Check it.">
    **Keep the escape path clear.**
  </Check>
  <Check id="watch" why="The sawyer is looking at the bar. You are looking at everything else: the crown above him, the root ball behind him, the log that just started to move, the neighbor walking up the driveway.">
    **Watch the sawyer's back, and watch above him.**
  </Check>
  <Check id="stop" why="You are the one person who can see the whole picture. If you see something, stop the work. You do not need to be right — you need to be heard.">
    **Call the stop.**
  </Check>
</Checklist>

<Hazard label="What happens without a swamper">
A sawyer working alone buries himself in his own cuts.

The pieces pile up around his feet, so his footing goes. The pile hides the
log, so he can no longer see what is holding what up. He starts reaching over
debris to make cuts, which puts the bar in front of his body and his feet in
the wrong place. The escape route fills with brush he threw there himself.

Then something moves, and there is nowhere to go.

Almost every "how did that even happen" injury story starts with a sawyer
working in a pile of his own making.
</Hazard>

</Block>

<Block kicker="The nod">

This is a hard rule and it is worth treating as absolute.

**The swamper does not enter the sawyer's work zone until the sawyer makes
eye contact and nods him in. Before the swamper steps in, the saw is idled or
shut off, and the chain brake is on.**

No exceptions. Not to grab one piece. Not because the sawyer is obviously
finishing a cut. Not because you shouted first.

<StopWork trigger="your partner in your zone uninvited" who="a reset — both of you step back and start the cycle again">
If you look up and your partner is inside your work zone and you did not nod
him in, stop the saw. Do not finish the cut. This is the one discipline that
breaks quietly and the consequences are not recoverable.
</StopWork>

**Why eye contact and not shouting.** You cannot hear over a running saw
through hearing protection, which means every shouted instruction is a guess
about whether it was heard. Eye contact is confirmation. It is the only
confirmation available.

**Why the saw stops first.** Because the swamper is about to be inside the
radius of the bar, bent over, with his hands in the wood, and because
somebody is going to stumble on storm ground eventually.

</Block>

<Block kicker="Hand signals that work over a running saw">

Agree on these before you start. Big, slow, repeated until acknowledged.

| Signal | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Flat hand held up, held still | **Stop. Now.** Kill the saw. |
| Both hands crossed overhead | Stop and come to me — something is wrong |
| Point at eyes, then point up | Look up. Something above you. |
| Tap the top of your head | Coming in — request to enter the zone |
| Nod, with eye contact | Approved, come in (saw already braked) |
| Thumb across the throat | Shut the saw down when you finish this cut |
| Point, then sweep the arm | That piece goes that way |

The only one that matters is the first. Everyone on site, including
bystanders and the homeowner, gets told what the flat hand means before work
starts.

</Block>

<Block kicker="The pre-work briefing, out loud, every job">

Ninety seconds, standing at the truck, before anything comes out of it. Say
it out loud even if you have worked together for years — the point is that
both of you hear the same plan.

1. **The plan.** What we are cutting, in what order, and what we are leaving.
2. **The hazards.** Lines, root ball, spring poles, hangers, the neighbor's
   fence, what the ground is like, bees.
3. **The escape routes.** Where they are, and are they cleared.
4. **The stop-work signal.** Flat hand. Anyone can call it, including the
   homeowner.
5. **Who calls 911**, and the location we would give them.
6. **Where the vehicle is**, whether the keys are in it, and whether the route
   out is still clear.
7. **What we are not doing today.** Say the refusals out loud at the start.
   It is much harder to talk yourself into a hung-up tree at 3pm if you named
   it at 8am.

</Block>

<FailureModes>
  <Fail tell="Both saws running, twenty feet apart, both people looking down. Nobody is watching anybody.">
    Two people, two saws, no swamper.
  </Fail>
  <Fail tell="The pile of cut rounds around the sawyer's feet gets deeper and nobody moves it because everyone is cutting.">
    Letting the work face close up.
  </Fail>
  <Fail tell="The swamper starts grabbing pieces while the saw is running because it is faster. It is faster, and it works, until the day it doesn't.">
    The nod quietly eroding over a long day.
  </Fail>
  <Fail tell="Four tanks in, one person has done all the cutting and is now making sloppy cuts while the other one is fresh and bored.">
    Skipping the rotation because the sawyer is 'in the rhythm'.
  </Fail>
  <Fail tell="Neither of you can remember what you agreed about the hangers because you never said it out loud.">
    Skipping the briefing because you know each other.
  </Fail>
</FailureModes>
