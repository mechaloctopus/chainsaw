Most of what a storm crew does is bucking and limbing. It is also where most
of the injuries are, because it looks like the easy part and it is done
hundreds of times a day when people are tired.

<Block kicker="Before the first cut on any log">

1. **Where is it supported?** Walk its length and look underneath at ground
   level.
2. **Which way will each piece move** when it comes free — roll, drop, swing,
   or spring?
3. **Where am I standing**, and where do I go if it moves? Uphill, and out of
   the roll line.
4. **Is there metal in it?** Old fence, nails, cable, a swelling in the bark.
5. **What is above me?** Look up.

</Block>

<Block kicker="Reading the bind, and the cut order">

<BindOrder />

**Compression cut first.** A shallow cut into the compression side —
typically a third of the way through — relieves the squeeze and stops the kerf
closing on the bar.

**Tension cut second.** Then come in from the tension side to meet the first
cut. The piece separates cleanly and goes where you expected it to.

**Or bore it.** Where you cannot get at one side, or the load is big, bore the
bar nose into the middle of the log and cut outward toward the tension side,
leaving a holding strap to cut last. Boring is a nose technique — read
[kickback](/modules/09-kickback-pull-in-push-back/) before you try it.

<Hazard label="The rolling round">
A round cut free from a log on any slope will roll, and it will roll further
than you expect over wet grass. It weighs more than you do.

Stand on the uphill side, always. Never let your partner be downhill of a
cut you are about to finish. When you free a round on a slope, say so out
loud before the last inch.
</Hazard>

</Block>

<Block kicker="Limbing">

Limbing a downed crown is where the bar spends the most time near your legs.

<Checklist id="limbing" ordered={false}>
  <Check id="uphill" why="Gravity decides where everything goes when it comes free, and you want to be above it.">
    **Work from the uphill side.**
  </Check>
  <Check id="butt" why="Butt to tip. Working the other way means cutting limbs that are still under the load of the ones behind them.">
    **Start at the butt and work toward the tip.**
  </Check>
  <Check id="between" why="Where the shape of the tree allows it, keep the trunk between your legs and the bar. The log is the best guard you have.">
    **Log between you and the bar.**
  </Check>
  <Check id="across" why="Never cut with the bar crossing in front of your body. If it kicks or the limb releases, the bar sweeps through where you are standing.">
    **Never limb across your body.**
  </Check>
  <Check id="support" why="Every limb you cut is either holding the trunk up or being held down by it. Both are loaded. Cut the small stuff off the top first to see what is actually structural.">
    **Treat every limb as loaded until proven otherwise.**
  </Check>
  <Check id="clear" why="Cut pieces go to the pile, not around your feet. That is what the swamper is for.">
    **Keep your feet clear.**
  </Check>
  <Check id="brake" why="Every single step. On storm ground you will trip, and the question is only whether the chain is turning when you do.">
    **Chain brake on before you move your feet.**
  </Check>
</Checklist>

</Block>

<Block kicker="The brake-on-every-step rule">

It is the habit that separates crews who have had a near miss from crews who
have not.

You are on ground made of loose bark, cut rounds, wet leaves, mud and stobs.
You will lose your footing at some point today. The chain brake takes half a
second to set with the back of your left hand and costs you nothing.

Brake on: every step, every handover, every time you set the saw down, every
time you look away, every time your partner speaks to you.

</Block>

<Block kicker="A pinched bar">

It will happen. Handle it calmly and you lose ten minutes; handle it wrong
and you bend a bar or hurt yourself.

1. **Stop the engine.** Before anything else.
2. **Do not lever the saw.** The powerhead is not a pry bar, and twisting it
   spreads the rails.
3. **Wedge it.** Plastic wedge into the kerf above the bar, tapped with a
   hammer or the back of an axe, to open the cut.
4. **Take the weight off.** Lever the log with a pole or a bar — something
   that is not your saw.
5. **Or cut it out.** A second cut from another angle, with a second saw,
   relieving the load. This is the honest reason crews carry two saws.
6. **Never use a steel wedge or a sledge with a chain nearby.** Plastic only.
7. If the log is loaded such that freeing the saw releases it — stop and get
   the load supported first. The saw is cheaper than you are.

</Block>

<FailureModes>
  <Fail tell="The kerf closes on the bar halfway through. You read the compression side wrong, or there wasn't one where you thought.">
    Bucking from the wrong side.
  </Fail>
  <Fail tell="The last inch parts and the piece drops on your boot, or swings into your shin. You knew where it was going and stood there anyway because it was convenient.">
    Standing in the drop line for the last inch of the cut.
  </Fail>
  <Fail tell="A limb under the trunk releases as you cut it, and the whole trunk settles or rolls a quarter turn — with your bar in it.">
    Cutting a limb that was holding the trunk off the ground.
  </Fail>
  <Fail tell="Bar across your body, reaching to the far side of the log because walking around it seemed like a waste of time.">
    Cutting across your body to save three steps.
  </Fail>
  <Fail tell="A bright nick on the cutters and a noise like a grinder. There's wire in the log and there is more of it a few inches away.">
    Finding fence wire the hard way.
  </Fail>
  <Fail tell="Two steps with the chain turning, on ground you weren't looking at.">
    Walking with the brake off.
  </Fail>
</FailureModes>
